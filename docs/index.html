Will the river run low next week? A 7-day early warning for the Blackwater River

Mankirat Singh · October 2026 · GitHub · LinkedIn

When rivers run low, water suppliers have less to draw on and less room for error. I used 21 years of public Irish river-flow and rainfall data to build a model that warns 7 days before the Blackwater River at Mallow Railway Bridge, Co. Cork drops below its low-flow line, and tested it honestly on the most recent 3 years, which it had never seen.

1. How often does the river run low?

I use the Q95 as the low-flow line: the flow the river stays above 95% of the time, a standard low-flow measure in Irish hydrology. It is 4.720 m³/s here.

As a sanity check, the EPA's hydrometric register lists this station's 95-percentile flow as 3.667 m³/s, against my 4.720 m³/s (+29%). Some difference is expected, as the two cover different years.

2. When does it happen?

Low flows are a late-summer problem, peaking in Aug. That is when an early warning is most useful.

3. Why can we see it coming?

The river responds to rain over weeks, not days. Rain over the last 60 days relates most strongly to flow a week later, because groundwater keeps feeding the river long after the rain stops. That delay is what makes a forecast possible.

4. Does the warning work on unseen years?

The model was trained on 2005–2021, its alert level was set on 2021–2023 (treating a missed low-flow day as twice as costly as a false alarm), and it was then tested once on 2023–2026. I compare it with the simple rule an operator might use: "warn when the river is already below the line." That rule does well once a dry spell is under way, because low flows last for weeks, but it can only react. The real test is the start of each episode: does the warning come before the river drops? In practice the two work together, so the warning system raises an alert when either the model forecasts low flow or the river is already low.

What this means for a water utility

A warning a week ahead gives time to check storage, plan abstraction and prepare customer messaging before supply comes under pressure. The same approach can run on any gauged river with a nearby rain gauge, so it could be scaled across many supply catchments.

Limits and next steps

  • One river and one rain gauge. Next: repeat across several supply catchments and compare.
  • Uses past rain only. Adding Met Éireann's rainfall forecast should extend the warning time.
  • Does not include abstraction volumes or groundwater levels, which would sharpen the forecast.
  • The alert level favours catching low flows over avoiding false alarms (about 39 false-alarm days a year). In practice it would be set with the operations team, who know the cost of each.
  • The test covers 4 episodes, so the results are indicative, not final.
  • Rainfall comes from Cork Airport, not inside the catchment; a closer gauge should help.
